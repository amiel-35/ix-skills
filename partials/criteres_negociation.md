# Success criteria — negotiation

Read this when the deliverable leads to an agreement between parties: a commercial renewal, a
salary or collective negotiation, a political or legislative negotiation. It adds to the method of
the skill; it does not replace it.

## 1. What is the deliverable, what is the outcome

- **Deliverable** (fully testable): the preparation — mandate, scoring template, fallback analysis,
  concession plan, draft text, impact assessment — and, afterwards, the written agreement itself
  (does it say what was agreed, is it complete and consistent).
- **Outcome** (depends on the other parties): whether there is an agreement, its terms, how each
  party feels about it, whether it lasts. Every outcome line is a zone (floor, target), set in the
  mandate **before** the first offer. A floor fixed after the talks is not a floor.

Most lines of a negotiation plan are `relecture` or `terrain`. State it in the coverage: a loop can
check the arithmetic of the template and the completeness of the documents, not the agreement.

## 2. Weighted interests and a scoring template

Following Raiffa's negotiation analysis (Raiffa, Richardson & Metcalfe, *Negotiation Analysis*,
2002), each party's interests are turned into a scoring template: the issues, the possible options
on each issue, a weight per issue (summing to 100 for each party) and a score per option. The
template is additive (Keeney & Raiffa, 1976), which assumes the issues are independent; issues that
are not (price and duration, salary and working time) are scored as packages.

Lines:
- our template exists, with weights signed off by the one who gives the mandate, before the first
  offer (`relecture`; who: the mandate holder);
- the other party's template exists, every weight marked as an estimate with what it rests on
  (`relecture`);
- every option on the table, and every package proposed during the talks, has a score for each
  party (`mécanique` once the template exists);
- the arithmetic of the template is right (`mécanique`, `calc-check`).

## 3. Each party above its fallback

The fallback is what each party gets without an agreement — its BATNA, best alternative to a
negotiated agreement (Fisher & Ury, *Getting to Yes*, 1981). It is valued in the template's units.

Lines:
- our fallback is valued, with its sources and its costs (a switching cost, a delay, a strike, a
  rejected bill) (`relecture`);
- the other parties' fallbacks are estimated, marked as estimates (`relecture`);
- the agreement scores above our fallback (`mécanique` on the template);
- the floor of the outcome zone is compared with the fallback: a floor set above the fallback is a
  preference, not a walk-away point, and the mandate says which it is — otherwise the negotiator
  will walk away from a deal worth more than no deal (`relecture`; who: the mandate holder);
- the agreement scores above each other party's estimated fallback, or the plan says why that
  party would accept below it (`relecture`). An agreement a party would be better off leaving does
  not last.

## 4. A non-dominated agreement (Pareto)

An agreement is dominated when another option exists that is better for at least one party and
worse for none. Line: no package in the template dominates the agreement (`mécanique` when the
options can be enumerated; `relecture` otherwise, by someone who did not negotiate it). A dominated
agreement leaves value on the table that both sides could have shared.

## 5. A threshold for the least-served party, set in advance

Non-domination says nothing about how the gains are shared. Choose a sharing rule before the talks
and write a threshold for the party that gains least:
- **Nash's rule** (Nash, « The Bargaining Problem », *Econometrica*, 1950): among the agreements,
  prefer the one that maximises the product of each party's gain above its fallback; the threshold
  is a minimum distance to that point;
- **maximin, after Rawls** (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971): prefer the agreement that makes the
  least-served party best off; the threshold is a minimum gain above its fallback for that party.
  Rawls wrote the principle for the basic structure of a society, not for a contract; using it in a
  negotiation is an analogy, to be named as such.

Line: the rule and the threshold are written in the mandate before the first offer
(`relecture`); the agreement meets it (`mécanique` on the template). For a law or a collective
agreement, the least-served party is often one that is not at the table (see §8).

## 6. Felt satisfaction, measured afterwards

A party can score well and feel it lost — and that feeling predicts what happens next. The
Subjective Value Inventory (Curhan, Elfenbein & Xu, « What do people value when they negotiate? »,
*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2006) measures it with 16 questions on four
dimensions: the instrumental outcome, the self (did I keep face, act by my principles), the
process (was it fair, was I heard) and the relationship (trust, a good basis for the future). The
questionnaire is made available by its authors for research and teaching; check the terms for other
uses.

The inventory measures the negotiators, not the people they represent: for a law or a collective
agreement, the satisfaction of those it applies to belongs to the durability lines (§7).

Lines (`terrain`, after the agreement, each party separately, answers kept from the other side):
- each party's score on each of the four dimensions is within the zone set in advance
  (floor and target `[proposed]` on the questionnaire's scale);
- no dimension is far below the others for any party: a good outcome reached by a process felt as
  unfair is a warning, not a success.

## 7. Durability at 6 and 12 months

In a longitudinal study of job-offer negotiations, the subjective value negotiators reported
predicted their job satisfaction and their intention to leave a year later, while the economic
outcome did not (Curhan, Elfenbein & Kilduff, *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 2009). A second
study found that subjective value after a first round predicted better outcomes in a second round
and the wish to negotiate again with the same counterpart (Curhan, Elfenbein & Eisenkraft, 2010).

Lines (`terrain`, dated in the plan):
- at 6 and 12 months: the agreement is applied as written (deliveries, payments, salary changes,
  implementing texts published);
- no forced renegotiation, dispute, litigation or departure caused by the agreement;
- the relationship is still usable: the other party would negotiate with us again.

## 8. Unrepresented parties: who pays?

Every negotiation has parties not at the table: future employees under a grid, customers of a
supplier contract, taxpayers, people a law will apply to, the next team that inherits the
agreement. One line, always:
- the list of parties affected and not represented, what the agreement costs or gives each, and
  who reviewed it on their behalf (`relecture`; who: someone without a stake in the deal).
An agreement that satisfies everyone at the table by passing the cost to someone absent fails this
line even if every other line passes.

## 9. A simulated counterpart as hidden part

The best hidden test of a preparation is an opponent who did not read it. Give a judge — a person
or the `red-team` skill — the other party's estimated template, fallback and constraints, without
our plan, and let it play the other side. The author sees neither the counterpart's brief nor its
moves before the exercise.

Lines (hidden):
- over a simulated round, the plan's answers hold without crossing our floor;
- the counterpart's three strongest moves were anticipated in the plan, or the plan is amended;
- the counterpart, played from its own interests, finds a package both sides prefer to the
  plan's target (if it does, the plan's target was dominated).

## 10. By kind of negotiation

- **Commercial** (renewal, purchase, partnership): the fallback is usually priced (a competing
  quote plus switching costs); the relationship and durability lines weigh as much as the price.
- **Salary and collective**: the grid itself is a deliverable with many `mécanique` lines
  (budget, internal consistency, legal minimums, pay gaps by group); the negotiation with staff or
  their representatives is an outcome. Salaries are confidential: say who may see them.
- **Political and legislative**: many parties, coalitions, amendments; the outcome is a vote,
  then the application of the text. The text is a deliverable (each provision does what its
  explanatory statement says, the provisions it amends exist in their current version, no
  contradiction between articles), and its compatibility with higher norms (constitution, treaties,
  equality before the law when a threshold sorts who benefits) is reviewed by a lawyer **before**
  the vote, not only observed after a referral; the vote, its margin and the application are
  outcomes, and the unrepresented parties line is often the most important one.

## 11. Usual coverage

Low for the outcome: a loop can check the template's arithmetic, Pareto on enumerated options and
the completeness of the documents. Agreement, satisfaction and durability are `terrain`; the
fallbacks, the other side's interests and the unrepresented parties are `relecture`.
